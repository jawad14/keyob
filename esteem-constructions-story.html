How it began

Residential building is a coordination business.

A home extension looks like a construction job. Run one properly and you discover it is mostly coordination. A single project moves through a discovery call, a site visit, a quote, design, council approvals, construction and handover, and each stage involves different people, different documents and a client at home waiting for news.

Esteem Constructions has done this well for sixteen years, and their reputation reflects it. The published motto is simple: trust, clarity and quality. Clarity in particular is a promise about information, which makes it a promise about systems.

Generic project tools are built for generic projects. They do not know what a first-floor addition is, that approvals can stall for weeks, or that the same client will ask three times when the frame goes up. Spreadsheets fill the gaps, then quietly become the system of record.

The brief: one platform built around how this builder actually runs a job, so the whole business can be seen in one place rather than reassembled from memory each morning.

THE PICTURE EXISTED. IT WAS JUST SPREAD ACROSS TOO MANY PLACES.
What made it interesting

Construction software fails when it ignores the site.

Four realities shaped the whole design.

01

Two very different users

An office team works on a desktop with time to type. A site supervisor is on a phone, outdoors, often with one hand free. The same information has to serve both without either group feeling the software was written for the other.

02

Long projects, slow-moving stages

A residential extension can run for months, and approvals can sit still for weeks through no fault of the builder. The system had to make the current state obvious at a glance, including when nothing is moving and why.

03

The client is watching

Esteem promises clarity, and clients at home notice when updates go quiet. Keeping an accurate internal picture is the only reliable way to keep an honest external one.

04

Adoption decides everything

A platform that is harder than the spreadsheet it replaces simply will not be used. Every screen had to be faster than the habit it was asking people to give up.

How we worked

We learned the business before writing code.

Stage 01

Discovery on their terms

Time with the people doing the work: how an enquiry becomes a quote, what happens when a design changes, who needs to know when a trade cannot get on site. The aim was to map the real process rather than the tidy version drawn on a whiteboard.

Stage 02

Designing the data model

Deciding what a project is, what hangs off it, and how stages, documents, people and conversations relate. This is the least visible part of a custom platform and the part that determines whether it still fits in five years.

Stage 03

Product and interface design

Builtrax was designed as a product with its own name and identity rather than an internal tool. A team that is proud to log in uses the system; a team that tolerates it works around it.

Stage 04

Build in short cycles

Iterative development with the client in the loop throughout, so corrections happened in days rather than after a six-month reveal. Features were validated against real projects, not hypothetical ones.

Stage 05

Rollout and training

Deployment at dashboard.builtrax.com, with onboarding shaped around roles so each person learned the part of the platform they actually use.

Stage 06

Support and evolution

A custom platform is never finished, because the business keeps moving. KEYOB stays close so Builtrax keeps matching how Esteem works rather than slowly drifting away from it.

THE DATA MODEL IS THE PRODUCT
The turning point

Naming it changed how it was treated.

Internal systems usually get called "the system". The decision to give this one a name, Builtrax, an identity and a proper login screen was not decoration. It changed how everyone spoke about it.

A named product gets used deliberately, gets feedback, gets improved. An unnamed internal tool gets tolerated until someone quietly goes back to a spreadsheet. Treating the platform as a real product was the moment it stopped being an IT project and started being part of how the company works.

What this work taught us

Custom software earns its cost in the fit.

Off-the-shelf tools are cheaper on day one and often the right answer. They stop being the right answer when a business has a genuine operating method of its own and spends real money bending a generic product around it, or bending itself around the product.

A builder who has run 1,700 projects has learned how to run a project. Software should encode that knowledge, not override it.

“The best measure of an internal platform is whether anyone still keeps a private spreadsheet on the side. That is the real adoption metric, and everyone in the business knows the honest answer.”
