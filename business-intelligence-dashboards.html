The short answer

What is business intelligence?

Definition

Business intelligence is the practice of bringing data from the systems a business already runs into one consistent place, then presenting it so people can see what is happening and decide what to do. In practice that means consolidating CRM, ERP, finance, website and operational data, agreeing what each measure means, and building dashboards and reports on top of it.

The hard part is rarely the chart. It is agreeing definitions, so that revenue means the same thing in two departments, and building the pipeline that keeps the numbers current without someone rebuilding a spreadsheet each month.

Why it matters

Decisions get made either way. The only question is on what.

When data is slow or contested, businesses do not stop deciding. They decide on instinct and memory instead.

01

Late data means late reactions

A problem visible in week one and reported in week six has had five weeks to compound. Most of the cost of poor reporting is delay.

02

Conflicting numbers stall decisions

When two reports disagree, meetings get spent reconciling figures rather than acting on them. Agreed definitions are worth more than better charts.

03

Manual reporting is expensive and fragile

Someone senior spends days each month assembling a report, and the method lives in their head. That is both a cost and a risk.

How to tell

Signs your reporting has stopped keeping up.

Common before a business invests in a proper data layer.

  • →The monthly report is assembled by hand from several exports.
  • →Two departments quote different numbers for the same measure.
  • →Leadership asks a simple question and waits days for the answer.
  • →Reporting depends on one person who knows where everything lives.
  • →You can see what happened, but not early enough to change it.
  • →Each system has its own dashboard and nothing joins them up.
  • →Nobody can state the current definition of a qualified lead.
  • →Decisions get framed as opinions because the evidence is contested.

How we work

Agree the questions, then build the view.

Dashboards fail when they start with available data instead of with the decisions they are meant to support.

Stage 01

Define the decisions

What choices does leadership actually make, and what would they need to see to make them earlier? That defines the measures, not the other way round.

Stage 02

Audit the sources

Where the data lives, how reliable it is, how often it updates, and what it would take to join it together. Realism here prevents disappointment later.

Stage 03

Agree definitions

Documented meaning for each measure, signed off across departments. This is what makes the numbers defensible in a meeting.

Stage 04

Build the data layer

Pipelines that consolidate and refresh the data on schedule, so the view is current without manual work.

Stage 05

Design the dashboards

Role-based views built for scanning and acting, with the small number of measures that matter rather than everything available.

Stage 06

Review and refine

Dashboards age as the business changes. We review which views get used, retire the ones that do not, and add what is missing.
